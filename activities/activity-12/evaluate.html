Why SIFT+?

The CRAAP Test is outdated — here's why

The CRAAP Test was designed for a world where information was slower, less abundant, and easier to trace. Today, AI can generate convincing fake studies, social media spreads misinformation instantly, and even legitimate-looking websites can mislead. SIFT+ is built on research-backed lateral reading — the same techniques professional fact-checkers use — updated with specific guidance for academic sources, AI tools, statistics, and social media.

The 4-Step SIFT Framework — Start Here Every Time
S
Stop
Before you read or share — pause. Notice your reaction. Are you angry? Excited? Surprised? Strong emotions are a signal to slow down and check. Ask yourself: Am I being manipulated right now?
I
Investigate the Source
Before reading the content, spend 60 seconds learning who made it. Google the author, outlet, or organization. You don't need to read deeply — just check if they're who they claim to be.
F
Find Better Coverage
If a claim matters, look for other sources saying the same thing. One source is never enough. If only one outlet is reporting something extraordinary, that's a red flag. Find the original source when possible.
T
Trace Claims
Follow links and citations back to their origin. Viral content often distorts or misrepresents the original study, quote, or event. Ask: does the source actually say what this article claims?
Checklists by Source Type — Apply SIFT, Then Dig Deeper
🎓

Academic Journal Articles

Peer-reviewed research — still requires critical reading

1
Is it actually peer-reviewed?Search the journal name + "predatory journal." Use Beall's List or the DOAJ to verify legitimacy. A journal that accepts anything for a fee is not credible.
2
Who are the authors — really?Check if they have a real institutional affiliation (university, research org). Search their name on Google Scholar. Are they actually experts in this field?
3
Look for conflicts of interestRead the funding and disclosure section. Was this study funded by a company that profits from a positive result? That doesn't invalidate it — but you need to know.
4
Has it been cited or retracted?Search the article on Google Scholar to see citations. Check Retraction Watch to see if it's been pulled.
5
What type of study is it?A randomized controlled trial is stronger than a survey. A meta-analysis is stronger than one study. Single small studies rarely prove anything on their own.
6
Do conclusions match the data?Read the abstract and results, then compare to the conclusion. Overreach is common — authors sometimes claim more than their data actually supports.
🚩

Watch Out For Journals with very generic names, articles with no institutional affiliations, studies with tiny sample sizes making sweeping claims, and research that has no independent citations after several years.

🌐

Websites & Online News

Includes news sites, blogs, advocacy sites, government pages

1
Open new tabs — don't stay on the pageSearch the outlet's name + "bias" or "reliability." Check AllSides, Media Bias/Fact Check, or NewsGuard. This is lateral reading and it's the most powerful move you can make.
2
Who wrote it and why?Find the author's bio and other articles they've written. Is this an opinion piece labeled as news? Is there a byline at all?
3
Check the "About" pageLegitimate organizations explain who they are, what they stand for, and who funds them. A missing or vague About page is suspicious.
4
Find the original sourceWhen an article cites a study, tweet, or event — go find it. Don't trust the interpretation. Often the original says something more nuanced or different entirely.
5
Is this satire or parody?Check the site's footer or About page. Satire sites like The Onion are legitimate — but their content gets shared as real news constantly. Know what you're looking at.
6
How does the language feel?Credible reporting is measured. Look for inflammatory headlines, excessive exclamation marks, or emotionally loaded language designed to trigger a reaction rather than inform.
🚩

Watch Out For URLs with slight misspellings of known outlets (ABCnews.com.co), sites with no About page, stories covered only by one outlet, and content with no dates or authors.

🤖

AI-Generated Content

ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, AI images, AI-written articles

1
AI does not have sources — verify everythingAI models generate plausible-sounding text, not verified facts. Any specific claim — a statistic, a quote, a study — must be independently verified before using it. Never cite AI as a source.
2
Watch for "hallucinations"AI confidently makes up fake citations, people, events, and statistics. If an AI provides a study or quote, search for it directly. If you can't find it — it may not exist.
3
Check the training knowledge cutoffMost AI models have a knowledge cutoff date. They may give outdated information about current events, recent research, or ongoing situations without flagging it as old.
4
AI-generated images: use detection toolsLook for unnatural fingers, backgrounds, teeth, or reflections. Use tools like Google Reverse Image Search or Hive Moderation to detect AI-generated images.
5
What was the prompt?AI output is only as good as the question asked. Output can be biased by how the question was framed. Always ask: who prompted this, and what were they trying to produce?
6
Deepfakes: does the audio/video match?For video content, look for unnatural lip movement, lighting inconsistencies, or audio that sounds slightly "off." When in doubt, find the original source.
🚩

The Golden Rule for AI AI is a tool, not a source. Use it to brainstorm, summarize, or explore ideas — but always verify specific facts through primary sources before using them in any work. AI output requires human verification.

📱

Social Media

X/Twitter, TikTok, Instagram, Facebook, Reddit

1
Don't trust engagement metricsLikes, shares, and followers don't equal accuracy. Viral posts are often the most misleading. Popularity is not the same as truth.
2
Who is this account really?Check the account's full history — when was it created? What does it normally post? Is it a verified account? Parody and bot accounts often look real at first glance.
3
Reverse image search everythingPhotos shared during breaking news events are often from unrelated times or places. Right-click any image → search Google Images. It takes 10 seconds.
4
Read past the headline or captionHeadlines and captions are designed to provoke reactions. Many people share content without reading it. Always read the full post — and then check if it links to a real source.
5
Has this been fact-checked?Search the claim on Snopes, PolitiFact, or FactCheck.org before sharing. If it's circulating widely, fact-checkers have probably already looked into it.
6
Check the dateOld content gets recirculated constantly — especially around elections and disasters. Check when the original post was made, not when you saw it shared.
🚩

Watch Out For Screenshots of tweets (easily faked), accounts created recently during major events, "BREAKING" claims without links to legitimate news coverage, and content that makes you feel you need to share it immediately.

📊

Statistics & Data

Numbers, charts, graphs, surveys, and research findings

1
Where did this number come from?Find the original dataset or study, not just someone's interpretation of it. Government sites (.gov), university research, and established research organizations (Pew, CDC, WHO) are generally reliable primary sources.
2
What was the sample?A survey of 50 people is very different from one of 50,000. Who was surveyed? Were they representative of the population being described? Self-selected samples are often heavily biased.
3
Relative vs. absolute numbers"Risk doubles" sounds alarming — but from 1 in a million to 2 in a million is very different from 1 in 10 to 2 in 10. Always ask: doubles from what to what?
4
Is the chart manipulated?Check the Y-axis. Does it start at zero? A truncated axis can make a small change look dramatic. Are the time periods cherry-picked to support a narrative?
5
Correlation ≠ causationTwo things rising at the same time doesn't mean one causes the other. Always ask: what else could explain this pattern? Has this been tested?
6
How old is the data?Statistics become outdated quickly, especially for health, economics, and demographics. Check when the data was collected — not just when the article was published.
🚩

Watch Out For Unnamed surveys ("studies show..."), round numbers that seem too neat, statistics without confidence intervals, charts without labeled axes, and data that's been cited so many times the original source is lost.

🎥

Video & Podcast

YouTube, TikTok, Spotify, documentary content

1
Who made this and why?Investigate the channel, producer, or podcast host using lateral reading. What else have they made? Are they trying to inform you — or sell you something, or build a following?
2
Are expert claims checkable?When someone is introduced as an "expert," pause. Google their name + credentials. Have they published peer-reviewed work? Or are they just confident-sounding commentators?
3
Is footage or audio being used out of context?Take a screenshot of suspicious footage and reverse image search it. Search for the specific quote or clip elsewhere to see the original context.
4
Does it cite sources you can check?Credible documentary and educational content should reference studies, data, and interviews you can verify independently. Vague "research says" without specifics is a warning sign.
5
Is this meant to be persuasive?Video is emotionally powerful. Dramatic music, selective editing, and compelling visuals can make weak arguments feel convincing. Separate the emotional impact from the logical content.
6
Check the comments with skepticismComments can reveal important context (like "this is satire") — but they can also amplify misinformation. Comments are not fact-checks.
🚩

Watch Out For Long-form videos that build trust over hours before making key claims, documentaries that only interview one side, and content that makes you feel like you've discovered something "they" don't want you to know.

5 Universal Questions — Ask These for Any Source

When in doubt, start here.

Question 1Who made this, and what do I actually know about them?
Question 2What are they trying to get me to believe, feel, or do?
Question 3What evidence do they give — and can I check it directly?
Question 4What do other credible sources say about this topic?
Question 5What important information might be missing from this?
